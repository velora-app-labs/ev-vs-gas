I want to compare the true long-term total cost of ownership (TCO) of two vehicles.

Before doing any analysis, ask me for the following information:

1. Vehicle 1: Exact model year, make, model, trim, and drivetrain if known.
2. Vehicle 2: Exact model year, make, model, trim, and drivetrain if known.
3. Location: City, state, and ZIP code. Use this to determine local sales tax, registration costs, electricity rates, gasoline prices, EV fees, smog/inspection requirements, and other location-specific costs.
4. Insurance: Ask for my expected monthly insurance cost for each vehicle.
    * If I don’t know the insurance costs, estimate them.
    * When comparing an EV against a gasoline/hybrid vehicle and reliable insurance estimates aren’t available, assume the EV costs 2× as much to insure as the gasoline/hybrid vehicle, and clearly label this as an assumption.
5. Annual mileage: Ask me, but default to 12,000 miles/year if I don’t specify it.
6. Charging/fueling: For an EV, ask whether I will primarily charge at home, work, or public chargers. For home charging, use the local utility’s current electricity rate and include approximately 10% charging losses. For gasoline/hybrid vehicles, use a realistic local gasoline price, preferably Costco if available.
7. Ask whether I want to specify an ownership period. If not, use the current average length of new-car ownership in the U.S. and cite the source.

Then research the exact vehicles and calculate their expected costs over the ownership period.

Include as many ownership expenses as reasonably applicable, including:

* Vehicle purchase price including destination fees
* Local sales/use tax
* Depreciation and estimated resale value at the end
* Insurance
* Electricity or gasoline
* EV charging losses
* Scheduled maintenance
* Oil changes
* Filters
* Brake service
* Coolant and other fluids
* Spark plugs and other scheduled engine maintenance
* OEM-spec tire replacement using realistic tire life and current prices
* Registration and renewal fees
* EV-specific registration/road-use fees
* Smog/emissions inspections
* Expected repairs and wear items
* 12V/low-voltage battery replacement where applicable
* Traction/hybrid battery degradation
* Expected battery capacity/range remaining at the end of ownership
* Battery warranty and likelihood of replacement during the ownership period
* Any other recurring or one-time vehicle expense that materially affects ownership cost

Do not automatically count a traction-battery replacement as an expense simply because the vehicle is an EV. Estimate degradation, warranty coverage, expected battery life, and probability of replacement realistically.

For financing, opportunity cost, home charger installation, subscriptions, parking, tolls, and similar expenses, either include them when enough information is available or list them separately as optional costs rather than silently ignoring them.

Analysis requirements

Use current data wherever possible and cite reliable sources for vehicle specifications, MSRP, EPA efficiency/range, local electricity rates, gasoline prices, taxes, registration fees, tire prices, warranties, maintenance schedules, depreciation/resale data, and other important assumptions.

Clearly distinguish between:

* factual/current prices,
* manufacturer specifications,
* historical data,
* and estimates/assumptions.

Don’t assume EVs are automatically cheaper or gasoline vehicles are automatically cheaper. Calculate everything.

At the end, give me:

Cheapest to operate: Vehicle X
Cheapest to own overall: Vehicle X
Difference over ownership period: $X
Difference per month: $X
Break-even point, if one exists: X years/miles

Then explain in 3–5 sentences why one vehicle costs more and identify the three biggest factors driving the difference.

Finally, tell me which vehicle is the better choice if my priority is:

1. Lowest total cost
2. Lowest monthly running cost
3. Long-term reliability
4. Performance/technology
5. Keeping the vehicle for 10+ years

Avoid false precision. If depreciation, repairs, battery degradation, future energy prices, or other costs are uncertain, show a reasonable range or sensitivity rather than presenting an uncertain number as exact.
